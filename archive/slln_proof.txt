\subsection{Understanding the Strong Law of Large Numbers}

To fully appreciate the mathematical rigour of the SLLN, we must investigate the mechanics of the drifting effect, covered in Section \ref{almost-sure-convergence}, and isolate the specific conditions that prevent it from continuing indefinitely. The fundamental idea that causes \textit{almost sure convergence} is contained within the bounds of probability limits. To explore this concept, we will need to understand two main areas, \textit{The Borel-Cantelli Lemma} and \textit{Kolmogorov's Formulation}, as well as cover the specific notation required for this section \cite[sec.~4]{probability1995measure}.

To establish \textit{almost sure convergence} for the SLLN, we must demonstrate that the sample mean eventually remains within an arbitrarily small margin of error $\epsilon > 0$. To formalize this continuous timeline, we define an event $A_n$ representing the occurrence where an individual sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ deviates from the true mean $\mu$ by at least $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
    A_n = \left\{ |\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon \right\}
\end{equation}

To measure the behaviour of these deviations over time, we use infinite unions and intersections. The infinite union, denoted by $\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty} A_n$, represents the occurrence of at least one deviation from index $N$ onwards. Applying an infinite intersection to these continuous unions, denoted by $\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty} \bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty} A_n$, creates a strict boundary dictating that no matter how large $N$ becomes, a deviation will still eventually occur.

As established by Billingsley \cite[pp.~52--53]{probability1995measure}, combining these operations constructs the limit superior of the sequence. This defines an event occurring infinitely often (i.o.):
\begin{equation}
    \limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n = \bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty} \bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty} A_n = \{A_n \text{ i.o.}\}
\end{equation}

This notation bridges the conceptual idea of drifting directly to our final proof. For the SLLN to hold, the probability of this entire limit superior must be exactly zero.

\subsubsection{The First Borel-Cantelli Lemma}

For the sequence of sample means to \textit{converge almost surely}, the probability that deviations occur infinitely often must be strictly zero. Having formalised this requirement as $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n) = 0$, we require a mechanism to prove when this condition is actually met.

The First Borel-Cantelli Lemma establishes that if the infinite sum of the individual snapshot probabilities converges to a finite value, the sequence will \textit{almost surely} stop deviating from the boundary. In Billingsley's formulation \cite[Theorem 4.3, p.~59]{probability1995measure}:

\begin{lemma}[First Borel-Cantelli Lemma]
If the infinite sum of the probabilities of a sequence of events $A_n$ converges, then the probability of their \textit{limit superior} (the event occurring infinitely often) is exactly zero:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(A_n) < \infty \implies \mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n\right) = 0
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\subsubsection{Kolmogorov's Formulation}

The First Borel-Cantelli Lemma provides the necessary condition to prevent infinite drifting, but proving that the sum $\sum \mathbb{P}(A_n)$ actually converges for a sample mean requires us to build further on the mathematics we have already seen.

It might seem intuitive to use Chebyshev's inequality similar to how we applied it in the WLLN. However, if we attempt to rely on this bound to satisfy the Borel-Cantelli condition, we encounter a problem with the rate at which it decays. Using Chebyshev's inequality, we find that the probability of a single deviation is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(|\overline{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \epsilon^2}
\end{equation}

To prove almost sure convergence, the First Borel-Cantelli Lemma strictly requires the infinite sum of these probabilities to be a finite number. If we attempt to sum the Chebyshev bound over all $n$ towards infinity, we can factor out the constants to reveal the underlying sequence:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{n \epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}
\end{equation}

The remaining sequence, $\sum \frac{1}{n}$, is the harmonic series. It is a fundamental property of calculus that the harmonic series strictly diverges to infinity. Because this sum does not converge to a finite number, Chebyshev's inequality decays too slowly to satisfy the Borel-Cantelli requirement. This dead end is exactly what necessitates a more powerful bounding method.

We can solve this by using Andrey Kolmogorov's much stricter maximal bound known as Kolmogorov's Inequality \cite[Theorem 22.4, p.~287]{probability1995measure}. For a sequence of independent variables with finite variance, Kolmogorov's inequality bounds the maximum partial sum rather than just the final sum:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P} \left( \max_{1 \le k \le n} \left| \sum_{i=1}^k (X_i - \mu) \right| \ge \epsilon \right) \le \frac{\text{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i)}{\epsilon^2}
\end{equation}

To apply this to the SLLN, the classical proof evaluates the sequence along an exponentially growing dual subsequence, $n = 2^k$. By applying Kolmogorov's inequality to this subsequence, we obtain a bound strong enough to control the maximum fluctuations between $2^{k-1}$ and $2^k$ \cite[p.~288]{probability1995measure}. This crucial step ensures that the sum of the probabilities over the subsequence converges to a finite number \cite[p.~289]{probability1995measure}:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P} \left( \max_{2^{k-1} < n \le 2^k} |\overline{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon \right) < \infty
\end{equation}

We can now directly apply the First Borel-Cantelli Lemma to the subsequence, which then extends to all $n$ \cite[Theorem 4.3, p.~59]{probability1995measure}. This proves that the sequence of sample means will eventually remain within the margin of error for all future observations:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n\right) = 0
\end{equation}

This equation states that, for any arbitrarily small margin of error $\epsilon > 0$, deviations will occur only finitely many times.

As this holds true for any $\epsilon > 0$, it implies that the sequence of sample means $\overline{X}_n$ will eventually remain within every possible bound around $\mu$. In the formal language of measure theory, the set of outcomes where the sequence fails to converge to $\mu$ has a probability measure of zero \cite[Theorem 20.5, p.~268]{probability1995measure}. Therefore, we can conclude that the sequence of sample means converges to the true population mean \textit{almost surely}, with probability 1:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P} \left( \lim_{n \to \infty} \overline{X}_n = \mu \right) = 1
\end{equation} \qed

Thus, we arrive at the formal mathematical conclusion of the SLLN, proving \textit{almost sure convergence}.
